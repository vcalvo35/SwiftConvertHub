Mbps vs MB/s: How to Calculate Real Download Times

📅 2026-11-03  ·  ✏️ Victor A. Calvo S.  ·  ⏱️ 6 min read
Last updated: November 2026  ·  Accuracy note

Your internet provider promises 100 Mbps. You start downloading a game and the launcher shows 11.8 MB/s. It looks like you are getting about a tenth of what you pay for – but in most cases nothing is wrong. The two numbers use different units: one counts bits, the other counts bytes.

Bits vs bytes

A bit (lowercase b) is a single 0 or 1. A byte (uppercase B) is 8 bits. Network speeds are traditionally measured in bits per second because data travels over a connection one bit at a time. File sizes are measured in bytes because computers store data in bytes.

  • Mbps or Mb/s = megabits per second – used by internet providers and speed tests
  • MB/s or MBps = megabytes per second – used by browsers, Steam, cloud apps and file managers

The capitalization of the b is the whole difference, and it is a factor of eight.

The ÷8 rule

MB/s = Mbps ÷ 8 and Mbps = MB/s × 8

A 100 Mbps connection can move at most 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5 MB/s. The 11.8 MB/s in the launcher is about 94% of the theoretical maximum – a normal result once network overhead is included.

How long will a download take?

Time (seconds) = file size (MB) ÷ speed (MB/s)

Example 1: a 5 GB game on a 100 Mbps plan. 5 GB = 5,000 MB, and 100 Mbps = 12.5 MB/s, so 5,000 ÷ 12.5 = 400 seconds, or 6 minutes 40 seconds in ideal conditions. Real connections lose some capacity to protocol overhead and congestion; assuming 90% efficiency, plan for about 7 minutes 24 seconds.

Example 2: uploading a 1 GB video on a plan with 10 Mbps upload. 10 Mbps = 1.25 MB/s, so 1,000 ÷ 1.25 = 800 seconds, or 13 minutes 20 seconds. Many home plans have much lower upload than download speeds, which is why uploads feel slow.

Add roughly 10% for real-world conditions. On fast plans the limit is often not your connection but the server you download from, your Wi-Fi or the speed of the disk you are writing to.

The second trap: GB vs GiB

Storage adds another source of confusion. Internet speeds and most file sizes online use decimal units, where 1 GB = 1,000,000,000 bytes. Windows, however, reports sizes in binary units – 1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes – while still labeling them "GB". A file advertised as 5 GB therefore appears in Windows as about 4.66 GB. Nothing is missing; it is the same number of bytes counted with a different unit. Our guide to digital storage units explains the IEC binary prefixes in detail.

Why your speed test and download disagree

  • Wi-Fi vs cable. Wi-Fi signal, distance and interference often cap real speeds well below the plan. Test with an Ethernet cable to see what the line delivers.
  • The remote server. A download can only be as fast as the server sending it.
  • Other devices. Streams and updates on other devices share the same connection.
  • Disk speed. On gigabit connections, an older hard drive can be the bottleneck.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many MB/s is 100 Mbps?

100 Mbps equals 12.5 MB/s, because there are 8 bits in a byte and 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5. In practice you will usually see a little less, around 11 to 12 MB/s, because part of the connection's capacity is used by network protocols. If you see a much lower figure, test with a cable instead of Wi-Fi and try a different download server.

How long does it take to download 1 GB at 50 Mbps?

At 50 Mbps the maximum transfer rate is 6.25 MB/s. One gigabyte is 1,000 MB, so the ideal download time is 1,000 ÷ 6.25 = 160 seconds, or 2 minutes 40 seconds. Allowing about 10% for real-world overhead, expect roughly 3 minutes. The same file would take about 40 seconds at 200 Mbps.

Why does Windows show a smaller size than the download page?

Download pages usually use decimal gigabytes, where 1 GB is one billion bytes, while Windows measures in binary units of 1,073,741,824 bytes but still calls them GB. A 5 GB file therefore appears as about 4.66 GB in Windows. The file is complete; only the unit used to display its size is different.
