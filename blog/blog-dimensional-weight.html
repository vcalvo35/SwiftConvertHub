Dimensional Weight: How Couriers Turn Box Size into Billable Kilograms and Pounds

📅 2026-11-03  ·  ✏️ Victor A. Calvo S.  ·  ⏱️ 7 min read
Last updated: November 2026  ·  Accuracy note

If you have ever shipped a pillow, a lampshade or an empty-looking box and been charged far more than its weight suggested, you have met dimensional weight (also called DIM weight or volumetric weight). Couriers sell space in trucks and aircraft, and a large, light parcel takes up as much space as a heavy one. Dimensional weight converts the size of a box into an equivalent weight so the carrier can bill for that space.

Billable weight: the larger of two numbers

Every parcel has two weights:

  • Actual weight – what the scale says.
  • Dimensional weight – the box volume divided by a carrier "divisor".

The carrier charges for whichever is larger. That figure is the billable weight.

The formula in inches and pounds

DIM weight (lb) = Length × Width × Height (in) ÷ 139

In 2026 the 139 divisor is the standard for UPS daily rates and FedEx in the United States, and USPS adopted the same divisor in July 2026, replacing its previous 166. Carriers typically round each fractional dimension up to the next whole inch and round the result up to the next whole pound.

Example: a box measuring 16 × 12 × 10 inches that weighs 6 lb.

  • Volume: 16 × 12 × 10 = 1,920 cubic inches
  • DIM weight: 1,920 ÷ 139 = 13.81 → 14 lb
  • Billable weight: the larger of 6 lb and 14 lb = 14 lb

You pay for 14 pounds even though the scale shows 6.

The formula in centimeters and kilograms

DIM weight (kg) = Length × Width × Height (cm) ÷ 5,000

International express services from DHL, FedEx and UPS generally use 5,000 cubic centimeters per kilogram. Some economy or freight services use 6,000, which produces a lower dimensional weight, and negotiated accounts may have their own divisor – always confirm the figure in your carrier's service guide.

Example: a box of 50 × 40 × 30 cm weighing 4 kg.

  • Volume: 50 × 40 × 30 = 60,000 cm³
  • DIM weight: 60,000 ÷ 5,000 = 12 kg (with a 6,000 divisor: 10 kg)
  • Billable weight: 12 kg, about 26.5 lb

Why 139 and 5,000 are the same rule

The two divisors look unrelated, but they describe almost the same density. One cubic inch is 16.387064 cm³ and one kilogram is 2.20462 lb. Converting 5,000 cm³ per kg into imperial units:

  • 5,000 ÷ 16.387064 = 305.1 cubic inches per kilogram
  • 305.1 ÷ 2.20462 = 138.4 cubic inches per pound

So a carrier using 139 in the US and 5,000 abroad is applying essentially one rule in two unit systems. Our first example confirms it: the 16 × 12 × 10 inch box is 40.64 × 30.48 × 25.4 cm = 31,462 cm³, which gives 6.29 kg – about 13.9 lb, matching the 13.81 lb from the imperial formula. How to reduce dimensional weight

  • Use the smallest box that protects the item. Shrinking the example box to 14 × 10 × 8 inches cuts the volume to 1,120 in³ and the DIM weight to 9 lb – five billable pounds less.
  • Measure correctly. Measure the longest point of each side, including bulges. Carriers re-measure parcels automatically and add a correction fee if your declared size is too small.
  • Use poly mailers for soft goods such as clothing, which don't need a rigid box.
  • Compare divisors. If you ship a lot of light, bulky products, ask about services or contracts with a higher divisor.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate dimensional weight in kilograms?

Multiply the length, width and height of the box in centimeters and divide the result by 5,000, the divisor most international express carriers use. For example, a 50 × 40 × 30 cm box is 60,000 cm³, and 60,000 ÷ 5,000 = 12 kg. The carrier then bills whichever is larger, this figure or the actual weight. Some economy services divide by 6,000 instead, so check your carrier's rules.

Why is the dimensional weight divisor 139?

139 is the number of cubic inches per pound that UPS, FedEx and, since July 2026, USPS use for US parcels. It describes the minimum density a package must have before it is billed on actual weight rather than size. It is almost the same rule as the 5,000 cm³ per kg divisor used internationally: converting 5,000 cm³/kg to imperial units gives about 138.4 cubic inches per pound.

Does dimensional weight apply to small packages?

For most US express and ground services it applies to any package size, although USPS applies its dimensional rules to parcels larger than one cubic foot. A small, dense item such as a book will usually be billed on actual weight anyway, because its dimensional weight is lower. Dimensional weight matters most for light, bulky items like pillows, lamps, packaging-heavy products and clothing shipped in oversized boxes.
